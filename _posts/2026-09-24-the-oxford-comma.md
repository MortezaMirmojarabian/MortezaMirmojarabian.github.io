Few punctuation marks inspire as much loyalty — and as much mockery — as the Oxford (serial) comma: the comma before *and* in a list of three or more items.

## What it is

- Without: *I love my parents, Lady Gaga and Humpty Dumpty.*
- With: *I love my parents, Lady Gaga, and Humpty Dumpty.*

The first sentence, read uncharitably, suggests your parents *are* Lady Gaga and Humpty Dumpty. The comma removes the ambiguity.

## When it matters

The Oxford comma shines when list items could merge into one unit:

- *She dedicated the book to her mentors, a teacher and a friend.* (one person or three?)
- *We invited the strippers, JFK and Stalin.* (the famous meme case)

## When it doesn't

In journalism, AP style drops it to save space, and most lists are perfectly clear without it: *I bought bread, milk and eggs.* Nobody is confused.

## My advice for learners

1. Pick one style (I use the Oxford comma) and **be consistent**.
2. Whatever your style, add the comma the moment a list becomes ambiguous.
3. In exams like IELTS, clarity and consistency beat ideology.

> Punctuation exists for the reader, not for the rulebook. If a comma prevents a misreading, it has earned its place.
