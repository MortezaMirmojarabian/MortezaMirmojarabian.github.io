Phrasal verbs are where English stops being logical and starts being fun. *Take* is a perfect example: add one small word and the meaning changes completely. Here are the ones you'll actually meet.

## Take off

Two main uses:

- **An aircraft leaving the ground:** *"The flight took off on time."*
- **Something becoming successful quickly:** *"Her online course really took off."*

It can also mean removing clothing: *"Take off your shoes, please."*

## Take up

To start a new hobby or activity.

> *"I took up photography during the holidays."*

Also: to fill time or space. *"My commute takes up two hours a day."*

## Take over

To get control of something.

> *"A bigger company took over the start-up."*

## Take after

To resemble a family member.

> *"She takes after her father, same humor, same stubbornness."*

## Take back

To return something to a shop, or to admit something you said was wrong.

> *"I take that back, you were right about the schedule."*

## Take in

To absorb and understand information.

> *"That's a lot of new material, let me take it in for a moment."*

## Take on

To accept extra work or responsibility.

> *"Don't take on more than you can handle."*

## Separable or not?

Many phrasal verbs are separable: *"take off your shoes"* or *"take your shoes off"* are both fine. But with a pronoun, the pronoun must go in the middle: say *"take them off"*, never *"take off them"*. With prepositional verbs like *take after*, you can't separate at all: *"she takes after him"*, not *"takes him after"*.

## Learn them in context

Don't memorize lists. Pick two or three phrasal verbs a week, and write one sentence about your own life with each. If you used it about yourself, you'll remember it.
