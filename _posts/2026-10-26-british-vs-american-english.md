Learners often ask which English is "correct": British or American. The honest answer: both. The two varieties are mutually understandable almost all the time, and most of the world now learns a mix. But the differences are real, and knowing the main patterns helps you listen, read, and sound consistent.

## Vocabulary

| British | American | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| lift | elevator | machine that carries you between floors |
| flat | apartment | place to live |
| autumn | fall | season after summer |
| trousers | pants | what you wear on your legs |
| biscuit | cookie | small baked snack |
| rubbish | trash / garbage | things you throw away |
| holiday | vacation | time off work |
| petrol | gas / gasoline | car fuel |

One warning: some words mean different things. A British *biscuit* and an American *biscuit* are different foods, and *pants* in Britain means underwear, not trousers. Context usually saves you, but be careful.

## Spelling

American English simplified some spellings in the 19th century, so the patterns are regular:

| Pattern | British | American |
|---|---|---|
| -our / -or | colour, favourite | color, favorite |
| -re / -er | centre, metre | center, meter |
| -ise / -ize | realise, organise | realize, organize |
| -ogue / -og | catalogue, dialogue | catalog, dialog |

Choose one variety and stick to it in a given document. Mixing *colour* with *organize* looks sloppy in formal writing, though readers will understand either way.

## Grammar

- **Present perfect vs past simple:** British: *"I've just eaten."* American: *"I just ate."* Both are correct in their variety.
- **Have got / have:** British speakers prefer *"Have you got a pen?"*; Americans prefer *"Do you have a pen?"*
- **Collective nouns:** British treats *the team are* as acceptable; American strictly uses *the team is*.

## Pronunciation

The biggest audible differences are the /r/ sound (Americans pronounce the r in *car* and *hard*; most Brits don't), the vowel in *bath*, *dance*, *can't* (long in Britain, short in America), and the *t* in words like *water* (a crisp /t/ in Britain, closer to a soft /d/ in much of America).

## So which should you learn?

The one you'll actually use: pick the variety that matches your exams, your teachers, and the media you consume. Consistency matters more than the choice itself. And when you hear a new word, just note which side of the Atlantic it comes from, so your English stays one English, not two half-learned ones.
