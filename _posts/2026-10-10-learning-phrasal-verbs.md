A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus a small word (a particle) such as *up*, *out*, or *off*. Together they often mean something different from the verb on its own: *give up* (stop trying), *look after* (take care of), *run out of* (have none left).

## Why long lists don't work

There are thousands of phrasal verbs, and many have several meanings. *Pick up* can mean to lift something, to collect someone, to learn something casually (*pick up a few words*), or to improve (*sales picked up*). A list cannot show you which meaning you need, but a situation can.

## Habit 1: Learn them in situations

Group phrasal verbs by the place or moment where you would use them:

| Situation | Phrasal verbs |
| --- | --- |
| At the airport | check in, take off, pick someone up, see someone off |
| At the office | set up a meeting, carry on, call off, hand in |

## Habit 2: Notice what the particle does

Particles often carry a pattern of meaning:

- **up**: increase or finish (*speed up*, *use up*)
- **down**: reduce (*slow down*, *cut down*)
- **out**: disappear or share (*run out*, *hand out*)
- **off**: start or end (*set off*, *call off*)
- **back**: return (*come back*, *pay back*)

These patterns are shortcuts, not rules. They help you guess, but always check.

## Habit 3: Check whether it can be split

Some phrasal verbs are **separable**: *turn off the light*, *turn the light off*, *turn it off* (never *turn off it*). Others are not: *look after the kids*, *look after them* (never *look the kids after*). A learner's dictionary marks this for you.

## Habit 4: Use them within a week

Write three sentences about your own day with the new phrasal verbs, then look at them again after two days and after a week. Seeing a phrase a few times, spaced out, is what moves it into your active vocabulary.
