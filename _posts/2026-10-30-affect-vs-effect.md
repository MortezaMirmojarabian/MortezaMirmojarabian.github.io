*Affect* and *effect* sound almost the same, look almost the same, and are closely related in meaning, which is exactly why they cause so much trouble. The good news: one simple rule gives you the right word nine times out of ten.

## The rule

- **Affect** is usually a **verb**. It means *to influence* or *to change* something.
- **Effect** is usually a **noun**. It means *a result* or *a change that has happened*.

| Word | Part of speech | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| affect | verb | to influence | The noise **affected** my concentration. |
| effect | noun | a result | The noise had a bad **effect** on my concentration. |

## A memory trick

**A**ffect is an **A**ction (a verb). **E**ffect is the **E**nd result (a noun).

You can also swap in a synonym to test yourself:

- Smoking can **affect** your health. You could say *influence your health*, so it is *affect*.
- Smoking has a harmful **effect** on your health. You could say *a harmful result*, so it is *effect*.

## The exceptions (for the curious)

You will meet these two in more formal English:

- **Effect as a verb** means *to bring about*: "The new manager hopes to **effect** real change." She wants to *cause* the change.
- **Affect as a noun** is a psychology term for visible emotion: "The patient showed a flat **affect**."

If you are learning English for everyday life, you can safely ignore these two uses for now. You will recognize them when you see them.

## Try it yourself

Choose the correct word:

1. The new rule will (affect / effect) everyone in the department.
2. What (affect / effect) did the medicine have on you?
3. Lack of sleep can seriously (affect / effect) your memory.
4. The special (affects / effects) in the film were amazing.
