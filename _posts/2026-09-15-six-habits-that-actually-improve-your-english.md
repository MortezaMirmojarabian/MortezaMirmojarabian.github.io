After years of teaching, I've noticed that the students who progress fastest aren't the most talented — they're the ones with the best habits. Here are six that work, in order of impact.

## 1. Read something you'd read in your own language

Motivation survives only when the content is genuinely interesting. Football blogs, recipes, thrillers — the genre doesn't matter; the daily contact does.

## 2. Keep a "phrase notebook", not a word list

Words live in company. Write *make a decision*, not *decision*. Collocations are what make you sound natural rather than translated.

## 3. Speak to yourself narratively

Describe what you're doing while cooking or commuting: *I'm chopping onions, and my eyes are watering.* Silly? Yes. Effective? Extremely — it builds fluency without an audience.

## 4. Shadow one minute of audio daily

Play a short clip, pause, imitate the rhythm and melody exactly. One minute a day beats an hour once a week.

## 5. Write three sentences about your day

Then fix yesterday's three sentences. Editing your own output teaches you more than ten grammar exercises.

## 6. Embrace the awkward phase

Mistakes are data, not verdicts. Every error you get corrected on is a rule your brain finally files correctly.

> Fluency isn't a destination you reach one day; it's a side effect of showing up repeatedly.
