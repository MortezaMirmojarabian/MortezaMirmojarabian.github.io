Here is an uncomfortable fact about learning words: if you meet a new word once, most of it will be gone within a week. Psychologists have measured this for more than a century — memory for new material fades fast unless it is *revisited*.

So the real question is not *how many words can I study tonight?* It is *what makes a word stick?* These five habits are the answer.

## 1. Learn in chunks, not isolated words

Your brain stores language in patterns, so give it patterns. Learn *heavy rain*, not *rain = باران شدید*. Learn *take a photo*, *make a mistake*, *have a shower* — the words that travel together.

| Naturally | Sounds foreign |
| --- | --- |
| heavy rain | <span class="x">strong rain</span> |
| take a photo | <span class="x">do a photo</span> |
| make a mistake | <span class="x">do a mistake</span> |
| a strong accent | <span class="x">a powerful accent</span> |

A word in a chunk comes with its grammar pre-attached. That is why learners who study phrases sound fluent sooner than learners who study single words.

## 2. Space out your reviews

Ten minutes of review on five different days beats fifty minutes on one day. Each successful recall slows down the fading. A simple, proven schedule looks like this:

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| Day 1 | Meet the word (in an article, in class) |
| Day 2 | First review |
| Day 4 | Second review |
| Day 8 | Third review |
| Day 15 | Fourth review |
| Day 30 | Final check — it's yours |

You can do this with flashcards or a notebook; the *timing* matters more than the tool.

## 3. Test yourself — don't reread

Rereading a list feels productive and does almost nothing. *Trying to remember* — and finding out whether you were right — is what builds memory. Cover the meanings and force yourself to recall them. Even a failed attempt, once corrected, sticks far better than a passive glance.

This is exactly how the exercises on this site work: instant, honest feedback after every question, so your brain gets the correction at the moment it matters.

## 4. Write your own example sentence

Dictionary examples are forgettable because they are about strangers. Your examples should be about *you*: *My brother-in-law is quite stubborn.* Personal, slightly emotional, a little funny — that is what memory loves.

One sentence per word, in your own notebook. Thirty seconds of work that multiplies everything else.

## 5. Keep a "mistakes I fixed" list

Every time someone corrects you — a teacher, a friend, an exercise — write the corrected version down. Your own past errors are the most personal vocabulary list you will ever own, and reviewing them protects you from repeating exactly the mistakes *you* are prone to make.
