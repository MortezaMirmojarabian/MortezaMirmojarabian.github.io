If there's one verb that powers everyday English, it's **get**. Native speakers use it constantly, and most of its phrasal verbs are easy to learn in context. Here are the essentials.

<!--more-->

## Movement & arrival

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| get up | rise from bed | I **get up** at 7 every morning. |
| get on | board (a bus, train) | We **got on** the wrong bus! |
| get off | leave (a bus, train) | **Get off** at the next stop. |
| get in(to) | enter a car / a place | She **got into** the taxi. |
| get back | return | When did you **get back** home? |

## Relationships & communication

- **get on with** — have a good relationship: *Do you **get on with** your colleagues?*
- **get over** — recover from (illness, shock, a breakup): *It took me weeks to **get over** the flu.*
- **get along** — similar to *get on with* (US): *They **get along** well.*

## Understanding & progress

- **get across** — communicate successfully: *The teacher explained it clearly and the idea **got across**.*
- **get through** — finish / endure: *I **got through** all my emails.*

## Change of state

- **get better/worse** — improve / deteriorate
- **get used to** — become accustomed: *You'll **get used to** the weather.*

## Mini-story

> Tom **got up** late, **got on** the metro, and **got off** near the office. He didn't **get on with** his new manager at first, but they soon **got along** fine. He never quite **got used to** the early mornings, though.

Try writing four or five sentences of your own day using *get* — it's the fastest way to make these stick.
